A quantitative research programme · ten investigations · one thesis

The Two Axes

Ten experiments asked whether markets can be predicted. They agree on which axis holds the answer, which one only looks like it does — and that the answer, where it exists, is always the same kind of thing.

calibration · patterns · adaptive filters · risk & regimes · a detection reframe · the variance premium · factor rotation · sentiment · FX carry · opening-range scalping

The question

Every price series has two things to say. One is its direction — where it goes next. The other is its volatility — how violently it gets there. We spent ten projects testing which one you can actually hear, under a single rule: assume any good result is a bug until it survives walk-forward evaluation, held-out data, permutation nulls, and every attempt to break it.

The answer came out cleanly split along those two axes — and then a third finding surfaced beneath it: the handful of edges that survived are all the same species of thing. What we learned about method proved as durable as what we learned about markets.

The arc

Ten routes to the same wall — and the doors that opened

Each project attacked the problem from a different angle. Read in order, they are a single argument: direction resists every method thrown at it, while volatility — and the risk premia around it — yields to the plain ones.

01monte-carlo · calibration

Envelopes, not forecasts

Bootstrap recent returns into forward price envelopes; judge them by whether reality lands inside.

worksFiltered Historical Simulation gives honestly-calibrated tails across 193 series.
failsDrift is pinned to zero — and stays there. No resampling beats a one-line vol model on direction.
02pattern-discovery · direction

Do chart patterns predict?

Discover price-shape patterns from data rather than hand-drawing them; test out-of-sample.

worksPatterns carry a statistically real signal (z = 2.4); amplitude beats shape.
failsEconomically negligible — IC ≈ 0.006. Unsupervised chart patterns are provably meaningless.
03adaptive filters · volatility

Self-tuning vol forecasts

A hierarchy of adaptive filters and a mixture of experts, forecasting realised volatility.

worksA fixed signed stack of diverse experts forecasts vol well (skill 0.34).
failsEvery adaptive, nested, self-tuning scheme loses to a single fixed fit.
04vol-risk · risk & regimes

Size by risk, not by view

Use the volatility forecast — not direction — to detect regimes, size positions, build a book.

worksA causal regime detector predicts forward vol at IC 0.60; the risk-first stack pays.
failsDirection bets stay cost-fragile; blind vol-targeting can backfire into rebounds.
05pattern-matching · direction, reframed

Ask detection, not average

Stop averaging shape over all windows; ask instead whether a real window is distinguishable from matched noise.

worksThe reframe finds it: real directional structure exists, beyond volatility (sign-flip control).
failsIt collapses onto known reversion; the book loses net of costs (Sharpe −0.30).
06variance-premium · monetising vol

Get paid for the forecast

Sell the variance risk premium — implied vol exceeds realised — and use the vol forecast to time it.

worksThe premium is real (+3.6 pts, 84% of days); short vol earns a modest Sharpe 0.36.
failsRegime-timing was a proxy artefact — on real futures it hurts. The return hides in the recovery.
07factor-rotation · cross-asset

Where does the money go?

PCA the cross-asset return structure into segments; ask whether rotating into out-of-favour ones beats holding.

worksA clean taxonomy: what is valuation-bounded reverts (asset classes, currencies).
failsWhat reflects who's winning trends (equity sectors, regions). No rotation beats buy-and-hold.
08sentiment · news proxy

Does the crowd's fear predict?

Test aggregate fear / risk-appetite gauges as contrarian signals — the free stand-in for news and positioning.

worksExtreme fear is contrarian — but only at the tail, and it is VIX (IC 0.15).
failsReal news / retail positioning is data-locked; the free proxies add nothing beyond VIX.
09fx-carry · currencies

What actually pays in FX?

Value, momentum and carry across sixteen currencies — and whether risk-management tames carry's crash.

worksCarry pays a real Sharpe 0.36 — the variance premium in FX clothing. Currencies revert (taxonomy holds).
failsYou can't time the premium for free — the timed Sharpe of 0.64 was a look-ahead; causally, 0.37.
10quick-flip-scalper · direction, both signs

A scalper from a video — fade, then follow

An opening-range fade sold as a “very good” strategy — box the first 15 minutes, short the liquidity-grab reversal — and its mirror image, breakout momentum, tested across the S&P 500 at 5-minute resolution.

worksBoth effects are real: a faint ~2 bps gross reversion, and a large opening candle that ignites momentum (a monotonic filter).
failsFade dies on costs (0/36 configs); follow was beta plus three trend days. Direction fails in both signs.

What all ten agree on

Five through-lines

01

Direction is a mirage — and reframing only sharpened the negative

Across bootstraps, chart patterns, a neural net, a term-structure of drift, cross-sectional selection, and finally a detection reframe, the information on direction never cleared the ~1% ceiling. Project 05 did prove that faint directional structure genuinely exists and is detectable — but it is reversion-shaped, mostly already known, and dies on contact with trading costs. Project 10 made the point from the other side: an intraday scalper failed as a fade (costs) and as its opposite, momentum (beta and three lucky days) — direction refuses to pay in either sign. A later daily-equity cross-check sharpened it once more: a proper mean-reversion entry, gated to the calm regime the programme says to trust, carries a genuinely significant, beta-neutral gross reversion edge (+0.28%/trade, CI clear of zero) — and ordinary dealing costs erase even that. Real, and still not enough. The momentum side of that same market was then tested to exhaustion — line geometry, volume, breakout extent, relative line age, volatility regime, and every combination — and each filter moved only how often a breakout wins, never how much: hit-rate is steerable, payoff is not.

02

The only edges are risk premia — never forecasts

Volatility is forecastable, regime-structured (IC 0.60), and the same series that hide their direction wear it openly. But the durable, tradeable edges the programme found — the variance premium (Sharpe 0.36) and FX carry (0.37) — turned out to be the same trade in two markets: sell insurance, collect a thin steady premium, and get run over in the crash. Never once did predicting direction pay. You are compensated for bearing risk, not for foresight.

03

Bounded reverts; fundamental divergence trends

When you rotate between things — asset classes, sectors, regions, currencies — a single rule decides whether contrarian or momentum pays. What is anchored to a value (commodity prices, yields, currencies vs purchasing-power parity) reverts, so buying the beaten-down works. What reflects who is genuinely winning (which sectors, which countries) trends, so momentum works. The same idea flips sign depending on whether prices are tethered to something that pulls them back.

04

The real split is robust versus overfit — not complex versus simple

Sophistication paid for volatility (high signal-to-noise) and failed for direction (low). Everywhere the signal was thin, the plain thing won: a fixed fit beat every adaptive scheme, equal weights beat fitted ones, a one-line VIX gate matched a fitted forecaster, a move-size filter matched a machine-learned detector, and equal-weighting beat every clever sector rotation. Complexity is a tax you can only afford where the signal is loud.

05

Every edge was smaller, and stranger, than intuition insisted

The patterns that "complete" actually reverse. Short volatility and carry are paid not for dodging the storm but for holding through it. And a risk premium cannot be timed for free — you choose how much crash to bear, you don't earn extra for foreseeing it. More than once the exciting number was an artefact — a look-ahead, a data glitch, a proxy — and the honest answer was always the quieter one.

The two doors that opened — and they are one door

The variance premium and FX carry — the same trade twice

Two edges survived every attempt to break them, and they turned out to be a single idea in two markets. Selling variance: implied volatility runs above realised ~84% of the time, worth Sharpe 0.36 on real VIX futures. FX carry: long high-yielding currencies, short low, worth Sharpe 0.37. Both are sell-insurance premia — a thin steady income, and a violent loss in risk-off, when they crash together.

You are paid to hold through the storm — not to dodge it

The variance premium's entire return comes from the 10% of days the curve is inverted — the violent recovery just after a spike (a single such day earns a Sharpe of 2.1). Carry pays for holding high-yielders through the risk-off that punishes them. In both, every sensible-looking timing rule backfires, and a premium cannot be timed for extra return: risk-management only reshapes the tail — it halved carry's drawdown at an unchanged Sharpe — it never lifts the reward. You choose how much crash to bear; you are never paid for foreseeing it.

The discipline that kept it honest

A good result is a bug until proven otherwise

The programme's most valuable habit was distrust of its own good news. Four times the exciting number was an artefact — and the method caught each one.

look-ahead

A defensive-stack Sharpe of 1.14 was a one-day leak in a regime filter. Corrected, it landed at 0.59.

bad data

Part of a reversion signal was corrupt prices mechanically bouncing. A spike-and-reversal audit cut it from 0.033 to 0.023 — the conclusions held, the edge shrank.

proxy

A variance-timing Sharpe of 1.3 was an artefact of monthly proxy accounting. Rebuilt on the real instrument, it fell to 0.36 — and the timing edge reversed sign.

look-ahead

A VIX-timed carry Sharpe of 0.64 had sized each month by that month's own fear reading. Lag it one month — only what you'd have known — and it fell to 0.37, no better than naive.

Each catch cost a favourite result. Each one made the surviving result trustworthy. The negatives are half the value — and the discipline is the other half.

The benchmark that outlived every strategy

And buy-and-hold beat all of it

Set the risk-adjusted bookkeeping aside and ask the plainest question a saver would: would any of this have left me richer than simply holding the index? Across ten projects, no. Every attempt to forecast direction and trade on it lost outright to buy-and-hold — the swing trend-line method returned a median −83% where holding returned +251%; no cross-asset rotation beat holding US equities; and the intraday scalper never cleared its costs to enter the race at all. The market's own drift is the benchmark nothing we predicted could clear.

Two honest exceptions — and why they don't overturn it

Risk-adjusted, one book did beat its market. The defensive stack reached Sharpe 0.59 against a UK-market 0.22, at a shallower drawdown — buy-and-hold did not win on those axes. But it buys a smoother ride, not more terminal wealth, and even that edge faded across the sample: Sharpe by thirds ran +1.49 → +0.29 → −0.16.

The surviving edges were never racing buy-and-hold. The variance premium (0.36), FX carry (0.37) and market-neutral momentum (0.34) are near-zero-beta return streams — diversifiers and drawdown-shapers, not substitutes for a long equity position. Blended, they lift a portfolio's Sharpe; on raw return, holding still wins.

So the defensible top line is sharper than “nothing worked”: every direction forecast we tried to trade was beaten by buy-and-hold, and the only edges that survived were risk premia and risk-management tools — winning, at most, on risk-adjusted terms, never on raw return, and visibly decaying.

The one-line version

Direction is a mirage no machinery beats — buy-and-hold outran every strategy we traded on it. The only edges that pay are risk premia — sell insurance, hold through the crash — modest, honest, never a forecast, and beating the index on risk-adjusted terms at best. At every turn the true answer was smaller and stranger than the exciting one.

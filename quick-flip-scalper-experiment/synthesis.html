Experiment 10 · a strategy from a video, put to the test

The Quick Flip

Presented as a very good opening-range scalping strategy. Tested honestly across the S&P 500, it is a real reversion effect — and far too small to survive its own costs. Then we bet the other way, and that looked alive — until the statistics got hold of it.

502 shares · 5-minute candles · 60 trading days · 4,409 trades · pre-registered, cost-aware, adversarially tested

The claim

The quick-flip scalper fades the opening range. Box the first fifteen minutes; call it a “liquidity grab” if that candle is large; then, when price pokes outside the box and prints a reversal candle, bet on a snap back to the far side of the box.

It is a tidy, mechanical, entirely testable story — big players engineer a shakeout, then price reverts. The pitch was that this works well. So we built it exactly as described and let the data answer, under one rule borrowed from the rest of this programme: assume any good result is a bug until it survives every attempt to break it.

The rig

A fair test, not a highlight reel

Every S&P 500 name, every trading day in the window, run through one mechanical simulator. Parameters were fixed before looking at results, not tuned to flatter them. Fills are deliberately pessimistic: when a single five-minute bar straddles both stop and target, we assume the stop filled first, because the bar hides the true path.

Liquidity filter as specified is nearly a no-op: the opening fifteen-minute range is a median 0.45× the daily ATR, so 93% of days clear the 25% threshold. It is not selecting rare engineered events — it admits almost everything.

The verdict

Break-even before costs. A loser after them.

Both directions, all names, first valid signal per day. Return is shown in basis points and in R — profit as a multiple of the risk staked at entry. The average trade risks about 39 bps, so a modest 5 bps round-trip cost is already 0.13R — and it is decisive.

Where trades end tells the same story: 45% stop out, 51% time out at the box edge, and the full-reversion target — the whole point of the trade — is reached just 2.4% of the time.

Why it fails

There is no reversion to catch

A fade needs price, on average, to come back. Measured on the actual bar extremes of every trade, it does the opposite.

01

Adverse excursion beats favourable

Median move against the trade is 0.86R; median move for it is only 0.72R. The typical trade goes the wrong way more than the right way — the reversion the strategy is built on is not there.

02

The target is out of reach in the box

Full reversion to the far side of C1 within seventy-five minutes happens 2.4% of the time. Most trades never get close, and expire at the time-stop.

03

A nearer target makes it worse

Taking profit at a fixed 0.5–1.5R instead of the far side lowers expectancy further: small wins can't offset full-size losses at a 40% hit rate. The problem isn't the exit — it's the entry.

04

The “liquidity” filter isn't predictive

Raising the threshold from 0.25× to 0.75× ATR removes trades without improving expectancy. The candle it flags as engineered is just a normal large opening candle.

The opposite bet

So we tried following, not fading

The excursion asymmetry was a tell: price ran further with the opening move than against it. So we inverted the thesis — an opening-range breakout that enters with the first break of the box, stops at the far side, and rides the continuation for the seventy-five minutes. And something changed.

The liquidity filter — inert for the fade — suddenly mattered, and monotonically: the bigger the opening candle, the stronger the continuation. At the strict setting it cleared costs.

For one afternoon, it looked like we had found something.

Under scrutiny

Three artefacts in a trenchcoat

A second hypothesis, on the same sixty days, chosen because the first failed — the textbook way to fool yourself. So before trusting a single basis point, four independent checks. It failed three, and the one it passed was the reason it failed the others.

01

Not significant, once clustered by day

The 1,928 trades are really forty-five correlated mornings — everything on a given day shares one market move. Resample whole days and the 95% interval on net expectancy is [−0.024, +0.060] R. It straddles zero. The flattering profit factor came from counting correlated trades as independent.

02

It was three lucky mornings

The best three days account for 156% of the total profit — every other day, net, loses. Remove them and expectancy is −0.011R. Day to day it is a coin flip: 22 up, 23 down.

03

Beta, not alpha

The window drifted down in the mornings (−3.3 bps), so shorts won for free. Market-neutralise — subtract each day's move in the trade's own direction — and the edge dissolves to −0.011R. The short side's strength was simply “shorts win when the tape falls.”

04

The one pass was that beta

A direction-permutation test screams significance (p ≈ 0) — but it only detects that the real direction beat a coin flip, which in a falling tape is the very beta the previous check removed. Not a signal; the confound wearing a lab coat.

Why we stopped here

Strip the three artefacts and nothing is left. This is exactly why we did not buy a month of out-of-sample data to “confirm” it: you do not spend money chasing an edge that has already evaporated in the very window that produced it. A hypothesis has to survive its own data before it earns new data.

The gap

Why the video looked good

The strategy isn't fabricated — the faint gross edge is real, and a highlight reel of winning fades is easy to assemble. Four ordinary omissions turn that into “a very good strategy” — the same species of self-deception we very nearly fell for one section ago, just pointed outward instead of in.

01

No costs

The P&L shown is gross. Our zero-cost column is mildly positive too — the video isn't lying, it's just omitting the line that decides the outcome.

02

Cherry-picked charts

A handful of clean hindsight examples where the fade worked, never the systematic sample. The 45% that stop out don't make the cut.

03

Discretionary lookahead

“Wait for confirmation,” “the right liquidity candle” — phrases that quietly use information the bar hadn't produced yet. Encode them mechanically and the edge evaporates.

04

Survivorship in the storyteller

You only hear the method from someone it recently worked for. The people it chopped up don't post.

What we can and can't claim

Dead here — not disproved everywhere

Direction failed both ways: fade it and costs kill you; follow it and the apparent edge is beta plus three trend days. One failed on economics, one on statistics — the burden of proof sat with each claim, and neither carried it. But the test has edges worth naming.

The caveats

One 60-day window and one regime, on a survivorship-biased snapshot of today's index. A more volatile regime could read differently.

We deliberately bought no out-of-sample data. The fade lost on costs; the one breakout variant that beat costs in-sample dissolved under stress-testing into beta and a handful of days. Confirming a dead edge on fresh data buys nothing — new data is for hypotheses that survived their own.

We tested faithful mechanical rules. A skilled discretionary trader might add context we didn't encode — but then it isn't this strategy, and it isn't testable as a claim.

The lasting product isn't the verdict on one video. It's the rig: any candlestick strategy can now be dropped into the same cost-aware, pre-registered, four-thousand-trade test and get a fair answer in an afternoon — a far better filter than a confident narrator.

Volatility is there. Direction, and the reversion it promises, still isn't.
